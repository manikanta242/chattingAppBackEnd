**Opening — Slides 1–4**

Today I want to walk you through a real, working chat application — not a toy demo, but actual production-style code, built with Angular 19 on the frontend and FastAPI on the backend. Before we dive into any one feature, let's get the big picture: the app is built from a set of well-known, specialized tools working together — Angular and RxJS on the client side, FastAPI and SQLAlchemy talking to a MySQL database on the server side, Alembic to manage database changes safely over time, WebSockets for real-time communication, and Groq for AI features. Keep these names in your head, because we're going to see every one of them in action. To tie it all together, let's trace what happens when you send a single chat message: it starts in the Angular app, passes through a piece of code that automatically attaches your login credentials, arrives at a FastAPI router, goes through a security check that verifies who you are, gets handled by a service that applies the actual business logic, and finally gets pushed instantly to the other person through a real-time connection. That five-step journey — client, security check, router, service, real-time push — is the backbone of everything we're about to explore.

**Section 1 — Authentication & Authorization (Slides 5–16)**

Let's start with one of the most fundamental questions any app must answer: how does it know who you are, and how does it remember you? The answer here is a JSON Web Token, or JWT — a digitally signed piece of text that proves your identity without the server needing to keep a memory of every logged-in session. This app creates these tokens with a library called python-jose, signs them with a secret key, and — cleverly — reuses that exact same signing-and-checking machinery for three different jobs: logging in, verifying your email, and resetting your password, just changing what's inside the token and how long it lasts. If you ever opened one up, you'd see it has three parts: a header naming the signing method, a payload carrying your email, your user ID, and an expiration time, and a signature that proves nobody tampered with it along the way.

Let's follow exactly what happens the moment you click "log in." The server checks your password against a securely hashed version — never the plain text — marks you online, and issues your token. The Angular app then saves that token to the browser, announces to every part of the interface that you're now logged in, and from that moment on, every request you make automatically carries your credentials without you ever thinking about it. Now, you might wonder — don't modern apps usually use two tokens, a short one and a refresh token that quietly renews it? This app makes a different, deliberate choice: one token that simply lasts a full week. That's a trade-off — simpler to build and reason about, at the cost of a slightly longer exposure window — and recognizing trade-offs like this is part of thinking like an engineer. All of this session data lives in one well-organized service on the frontend, which knows how to store it, rebuild it after a page refresh, and cleanly erase it on logout.

Now here's an important distinction: authentication asks "who are you," while authorization asks "what are you allowed to do." Some systems answer that second question with roles — admin, moderator, regular user. This app has none of that. Instead, it asks two simple questions every single time: are you a valid, verified account, and does this piece of data actually belong to you? That's a perfectly sensible design for a peer-to-peer chat app where everyone stands on equal footing. All of this is backed by one straightforward database table of users, where two fields do a lot of quiet, important work — one marks whether your email has been verified, and the other tracks whether you're currently online.

So how does the app actually enforce all this? On the frontend, two small pieces of code work as a team — a "guard" that blocks you from even loading a page you're not allowed to see, and an "interceptor" that automatically attaches your credentials to every outgoing request. On the backend, instead of remembering to add a security check to every single endpoint — which is easy to forget — whole groups of related routes share one dependency that protects everything inside automatically. At the heart of that dependency is a single function that does an enormous amount of quiet work: it pulls your token from the request, checks its signature and expiration, reads your identity, looks you up in the database, and hands your verified account to whatever code needs it — rejecting the request immediately if any step fails. And this same token-based approach extends naturally to email verification and password recovery, with one thoughtful privacy detail worth calling out: when you ask to resend a verification email, the response is always the same generic message, whether or not that address is actually registered — so the feature can never be used to discover who has an account.

**Section 2 — Real-Time Messaging (Slides 17–22)**

Now we get to my favorite part of this app: real-time messaging. Everything we've discussed so far has been a normal back-and-forth — you ask, the server answers. But chat needs something more immediate, and that's where WebSockets come in. A WebSocket is a persistent, two-way connection between your browser and the server — instead of constantly asking "anything new yet?", the server can simply push information to you the instant it happens. Remarkably, this app handles five completely different kinds of real-time events — chat messages, typing indicators, read receipts, online-status changes, and friend-request alerts — through just one single connection, kept track of by a simple lookup table living in the server's memory.

Let's trace exactly what happens the moment you open the chat screen: the server checks your token, accepts and registers your connection, marks you online in the database, tells everyone else you've just arrived, and shows you who else is already online — and when you leave, the exact same sequence runs cleanly in reverse. On the frontend, a dedicated service wraps the raw connection into something far friendlier — automatically reconnecting if it drops, intelligently avoiding retries when you closed it on purpose, and letting the rest of the app simply listen to a clean stream of events without ever touching the connection directly. Now let's put it all together and follow one message from the moment you hit send: the app transmits it over the open connection, the server validates it, saves a permanent copy to the database, delivers it instantly if the recipient is online, and sends a copy back to you so your own screen updates right away too. Notice that nothing is sacrificed here — the message is both saved forever and delivered immediately. And it's worth being upfront about the design choices behind all this: keeping the connection list in one server's memory is simple and fast, and letting the client manage its own reconnections keeps the server lean — these aren't flaws, they're sensible decisions for the app's current scale, with a clear path to evolve if it ever needs to grow.

**Section 3 — AI Features (Slides 23–28)**

Let's shift gears and look at how this app brings artificial intelligence into the experience. There are two AI-powered features here — an automatic spelling corrector and a conversational assistant — and both, remarkably, are powered by the very same underlying model, just configured in two very different ways. The autocorrect feature is a great example of constraining a powerful, general-purpose tool to do one narrow job extremely well: its instructions explicitly say "fix only spelling and grammar, don't change the meaning," its settings are tuned for very consistent output, and it only ever shows you a suggestion if the corrected text is actually different from what you typed — because nobody wants a tool that nags them for no reason. And notice this: even this AI feature passes through the exact same security check we saw earlier before the AI is ever consulted. Adding something flashy and new should never mean bypassing the safeguards you already trust.

The model behind both features is Llama 3.3, a seventy-billion-parameter language model running on a service called Groq, known for its speed — and crucially, it supports streaming, meaning the app can start displaying words as they're generated rather than waiting for the whole response to finish. Making that work requires some careful plumbing on both ends: the backend reads the AI's response piece by piece and forwards each piece immediately, while the frontend uses a lower-level browser tool that reads the response as a continuous stream of bytes, decodes it into text, and displays each chunk the moment it arrives — creating that familiar live-typing effect you've probably seen in modern AI chat tools. To sum up this section: this app ships two genuine AI features, both secured behind a login, treated as first-class parts of the product rather than gimmicks bolted on for show.

**Section 4 — Presence, Status & Notifications (Slides 29–37)**

For our final theme, let's look at how the app helps people stay aware of each other — who's online, what they're doing right now, and what they've recently shared — the things that make a chat app feel alive and social rather than just a messaging utility. You might assume that tracking who's online requires some elaborate separate system, but here it's refreshingly simple: it's just one field on your account that updates at exactly four moments — connecting, disconnecting, logging in, and logging out — and each change is broadcast only to your actual friends. Typing indicators and read receipts work on a different principle entirely: they're useful only in the moment, so the app simply relays them live through the open connection without ever saving them — a good reminder that not all data deserves permanent storage.

This app also includes a feature similar to "Stories" on social media — short posts that automatically vanish after twenty-four hours. What I love about the implementation is how elegantly the expiration is handled: rather than running a background cleanup process, the database simply records an expiration timestamp when the post is created, and any request for posts automatically filters out the expired ones. Friend requests offer a wonderful lesson in not reinventing the wheel: instead of building a whole separate notification system, the app simply checks whether the recipient is currently connected and sends them an alert through the very same real-time connection used for chat — one reliable mechanism, reused thoughtfully. The complete friend lifecycle — sending requests, viewing pending ones, accepting, declining, discovering new people — all flows through that same trusted authentication check, which is what makes a codebase feel predictable and safe to build on. And notice how the app chooses its communication channels deliberately: things that matter right now go out instantly through the live connection, while occasional, security-sensitive things like verification and password resets go out through email instead — choosing the right channel for the right moment is just as important as building the channels themselves.

Even something as ordinary as updating your profile turns out to reuse ideas from everywhere else in the app — the same security check, the same safe approach to naming and storing uploaded images, the same mechanism for serving them back. That's what a well-built codebase tends to feel like: a handful of solid ideas, showing up again and again in new contexts. And your profile isn't an isolated settings page tucked in a corner — it's the identity that every other feature refers back to, rippling outward into your messages, your status posts, and your friend list.

**Closing — Slides 38–40**

Let's pause and take stock of what's actually been done well here, security-wise: passwords are never stored in plain text, every token is signed and expires, accounts must verify their email before they can log in, protective headers are set on every response, routes can't accidentally be left unguarded, and every database query goes through a safe layer that prevents injection attacks. That's a solid checklist to carry into your own projects. And just as importantly, no system is ever finished — this one currently keeps its real-time connections on a single server, which works beautifully at its current size, but the natural next steps if it grew would be spreading those connections across multiple servers, adding a token-refresh mechanism, and introducing push notifications. Being able to honestly describe both what your system does well today and where it would need to evolve tomorrow is the mark of mature engineering thinking.

So if there are three things I'd like you to walk away with today, they're these: authentication and authorization don't have to be complicated to be effective — they just have to be consistent; a single, well-designed real-time connection can elegantly carry many different kinds of conversations at once; and being honest about your system's current limits, while having a clear plan forward, matters just as much as the code itself.
